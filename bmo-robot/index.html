BMO: The Line-Following Arduino Robot#

In fall 2024, I recreated the loveable gaming handheld character, BMO, from Adventure Time! This project lasted from late August to late November and was accomplished by an interdisciplinary team of two computer science majors, one computer engineer, one mechanical engineer, and one chemical engineer. This took place in ENG3000L, Foundations of Engineering Lab, at USF.

As the project lead, my main priority was ensuring the team prototyped and developed the robot smoothly, taking in everyones' thoughts and opinions and communicating with teaching assistants and the professor for instruction and feedback.

BB Era (Before BMO)#

Before the robot was realized into BMO, my team and I protyped our own robots made from cardstock (an "origami bot"). These robots were simple in design, had an open top, and were programmed to simply go forward, turn on each side, then go backwards. There were a number of issues, such as batteries weighing down the chassis too much, lack of chassis integrity, and inconsistent wire connections. Sometimes, the bot would even work.

However, despite these issues, it formed the base for the team's understanding of how to put together compontents together to form a working robot.

AB Era (After BMO)#

The next step for the team was to narrow down designs. It came down to two: BMO from Adventure Time, and the Krabby Patty Car from Spongebob. Since the project case was to design a robot for K-12 educational purposes, both designs came from cartoon shows to be as recognizeable as possible. Though we considered both strongly, we found that BMO would provide us with greater flexibility in technical features, but also a simplified design to avoid overengineering the chassis.

Final Prototype#

Once the candidate design was selected, my team and I finished the internals of BMO. One of the key steps was actually rotating BMO to be horizontal instead of vertical. While BMO is normally a character that stands, functionally, it left us with little room to insert circuitry. BMO was designed to have a shell system, where a shell would sit onto a base with tight margins to secure the shell to the base naturally. We also added a shelf insert to maximize BMO's vertical height. Other additions from the candidate are wider gaps for the wheels, a larger gap for the IR sensors for line-following, and holes in the base to ziptie the motors. Finally, the last addition: a pizeoelectric speaker. When BMO receives power, a startup sequence plays before BMO begins line-following. It can be heard in the video at the top of the page, but it is a bit quiet.

Changes Made Given More Time#

Due to the time constraints set by the course, we could not implement all the fixes for the final prototype that we had worked out. For example, BMO has a small footprint, but since time was low, we could not update the base size. Instead, we decided to increase the height of the shell to keep compatibility with the shell (an intentional design decision for situations like this). However, time was low and campus 3D printing was extremely busy, so we could not print the CAD design of the new shell.

Another change we were prototyping was the speaker startup sequence. While the sequence sounds great, it was played very quiet. Pizeoelectric speakers were challenging to understand in Arduino programming in terms of audio output, as it was partially tied to note choice. Increasing the volume would have required a complete re-do of the sequence, which in turn, required testing notes from a list of 50 or so. We had a great sounding sequence, so we left it as is.

Conclusion#

Overall, working on BMO was fun, rewarding, and a fantastic learning experience. I learned how to design simple CAD models, how to work with Arduino hardware and software, and how to lead a team of engineers. It also led me to be more interested in embedded hardware, something I had previously not dealt with.
